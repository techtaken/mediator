# Resolving a Dispute: A Mediator's Playbook

There is a proven path. Mediators, negotiators and conflict researchers have refined the same core sequence over decades. Below is that path, how to decide what to work on at each moment, and the mediator role written out as a reusable structure.

---

## 1. The proven frameworks

These are the well-established methods. Most real mediators combine them.

| Framework | Source | Core idea | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Principled Negotiation** | Fisher & Ury, *Getting to Yes* (Harvard) | Separate the people from the problem. Focus on interests, not positions. Generate options. Use fair, objective criteria. | Money, property, deals |
| **Facilitative Mediation** | Christopher Moore, *The Mediation Process* | The mediator controls the **process**. The parties control the **outcome**. | Most two-party disputes |
| **Transformative Mediation** | Bush & Folger | The goal is **empowerment** (each person regains clarity) and **recognition** (each understands the other). An agreement is a by-product. | Ego and relationship disputes, family |
| **Narrative Mediation** | Winslade & Monk | Each side has a "story." Help them build a new shared story that isn't "hero vs villain." | Long-running, identity-heavy conflicts |
| **Glasl's Escalation Model** | Friedrich Glasl | A conflict's stage of escalation determines what kind of help works. | Choosing the right intervention |

The one rule they all share:

> **People before problem.** You can't solve the substance (money, land) while emotions and ego are blocking the room. Calm things down, get each person recognized, and only then negotiate.

## 3. How to decide what to focus on, and when

This is the core skill of a mediator. There are three tools for it.

### Tool A: Glasl's escalation level (how hot is it?)

How far a conflict has escalated tells you what kind of help it needs.

| Glasl stage | What it looks like | Who can resolve it | Focus |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1–3: Win-win still possible** | Tension, debate, "actions not words" | The parties themselves, or a friend facilitating | Communication and clarifying facts |
| **4–6: Win-lose** | Recruiting allies, attacks on reputation ("he's a cheat"), threats | **A mediator** | Recognition first, then interests |
| **7–9: Lose-lose** | Deliberate harm, "I'll ruin him even if I lose too" | Arbitration, court, police, a power figure | Stopping harm. Mediation alone won't work. |

### Tool B: Root-cause category (what kind of dispute is it?)

Each category from Moore's Circle of Conflict has its own remedy:

| Root cause | What the mediator does | What NOT to do |
|---|---|---|
| **Data** (different facts) | Agree on a trusted source: bank record, a witness, the WhatsApp chat | Argue about whose memory is right |
| **Interest** (money, land) | Find needs underneath. Expand the pie: payment plans, swaps, timing. | Split the difference too early |
| **Relationship / ego** | Venting, recognition, apologies, reframing ("you felt humiliated") | Jump straight to the money |
| **Values** (beliefs) | Find a shared higher goal ("we both want the family together"). Agree to disagree. | Try to convert anyone |
| **Structural** (the situation itself) | Change the setup: boundaries, schedules, separate spaces, rules | Blame the people for a structural problem |

### Tool C: The focus algorithm (what goes first?)

Run this every time you're unsure. It applies at the start and throughout the process.

```
1. Is anyone unsafe (threats, violence, abuse)?
      → STOP. Protect. Refer to authorities or professionals. Do not mediate.

2. Is emotional intensity too high (shouting, crying, can't listen)?
      → De-escalate. Take a break or meet separately (caucus). Let them vent.

3. Are both willing and aware there IS a dispute?
      → If not, build readiness first: explain what each loses by NOT resolving it.

4. Is there an UNACKNOWLEDGED issue? (one side's issue the other dismisses)
      → Get it recognized first. It is BLOCKING everything else.

5. Do they disagree on FACTS?
      → Settle the facts next. It's the cheapest win.

6. Now order the remaining issues:
      → Start with an EASY shared issue to build momentum,
        UNLESS one issue is blocking the other person from listening. Then do that one first.

7. Work through INTERESTS → OPTIONS → CRITERIA → AGREEMENT.

8. VALUES differences: don't resolve them. Find a way to coexist around them.
```

> **Golden heuristic:** *Focus first on whatever is stopping the other person from listening.* Usually that's an unrecognized hurt, not the money.

## 4. The mediator as a structured role

Yes, the role can be written out. Think of the mediator as a **function**: it takes the Dispute object from earlier, diagnoses it, picks a strategy, runs the stages and outputs an agreement.

```
Mediator(Dispute) → Diagnosis → Strategy → Process (stages) → Agreement + Follow-up
```

### 4.1 Principles (the mediator's constraints)

| Principle | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Impartiality** | Doesn't take sides, and is *seen* as not taking sides |
| **Self-determination** | The parties decide the outcome. The mediator doesn't impose it. |
| **Voluntariness** | Anyone can walk away |
| **Confidentiality** | What's said in mediation stays there |
| **Process ownership** | The mediator controls *how* they talk, not *what* they decide |

### 4.2 Mediator styles (choose one for the scenario)

| Style | The mediator… | Use when |
|---|---|---|
| **Facilitative** | Asks questions and guides the process. Gives no opinions. | Default, and when the parties are capable |
